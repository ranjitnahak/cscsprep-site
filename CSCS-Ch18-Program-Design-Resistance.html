Topic 01 — Principles · 1 of 6

Specificity — Train Specific, Adapt Specific

What It Is
Train in a specific manner → get a specific adaptation (DeLorme, 1945).
Also called SAID — specific adaptation to imposed demands.
How It Works
Covers muscles used, muscle action (concentric/eccentric/isometric), and movement velocity/coordination.
Doesn't mean mimicking the sport skill directly.
Training Implication
Squat → relevant to vertical jump (same muscles, similar pattern) despite different speed/force profile.
Progress general → specific across preseason → in-season → postseason.
Topic 01 — Principles · 2 of 6

Overload — Beyond the Accustomed Stimulus

What It Is
Stimulus beyond what the athlete's used to → adaptation beyond baseline.
How It Works
↑ load, sets, or reps (volume); ↑ sessions/week; vary exercise choice or rest periods.
A reduction can also be overload (e.g. a deload week).
Training Implication
No overload → even a well-designed program limits improvement.
Deload week: ↓ volume, maintain/↑ intensity → performance rebound (fitness-fatigue paradigm).
Topic 01 — Principles · 3 of 6

Progression — Sequential & Systematic Increase

What It Is
Load/volume rise sequentially, matched to the desired adaptation → long-term benefit.
How It Works
↑ sets, sessions/week, exercises, or the technical complexity of drills.
Based on the athlete's needs and status — introduced gradually.
Training Implication
e.g. front squat → hang power clean → power clean (segmented positional progression).
↑ volume emphasizes hypertrophy; ↑ intensity emphasizes strength adaptations.
Topic 01 — Principles · 4 of 6

Variation — Removing Linearity

What It Is
Manipulates overload + specificity to remove linearity and drive adaptation.
How It Works
Modify volume, intensity, set config, rest, or exercise choice — at macro (periodized plan) or micro (session) level.
Cuts training monotony, adds a range of stimuli.
Training Implication
Heavy + light intensities combined → greater max/rapid force gains than one method alone.
Topic 01 — Principles · 5 of 6

Reversibility — Use It or Lose It

What It Is
Loss of a fitness quality when the training stimulus is removed, or fatigue is poorly managed.
How It Works
e.g. ↓ rapid force production during a high-volume hypertrophy phase.
e.g. muscle atrophy during a taper (reduced training volume).
Training Implication
Retaining loads (a small % of volume) can offset this loss — different from the minimal dose needed just to maintain a quality.
Topic 01 — Principles · 6 of 6

Individualization — No Cookie-Cutter Programs

What It Is
Training goals and needs are unique to each athlete — genetics + training history shape the response to stimuli.
How It Works
Avoid a cookie-cutter approach.
≈80%–90% of exercises used may be similar across athletes; 10%–20% individualized.
Training Implication
Loads are ALWAYS individualized to the athlete's capability, even when exercise selection overlaps.

Topic 02 — Needs Analysis · 1 of 4

Evaluation of the Sport

What It Is
Movement analysis: body/limb patterns & muscular involvement.
Physiological analysis: strength, power, hypertrophy & muscular endurance priorities.
Injury analysis: common sites/mechanisms of injury, especially non-contact.
How It Works
Analyze the sport's demands before choosing exercises — e.g. shot put: all-body extension pattern, triceps/delts/glutes/quads/plantar flexors.
Training Implication
Shot put → high strength + power need, hypertrophy beneficial, minimal endurance need, overuse risk at shoulder/elbow.
Collision sports (football, rugby) → hypertrophy protective + adds momentum (mass × velocity).
Topic 02 — Needs Analysis · 2 of 4

Training Status Classification

What It Is
The athlete's readiness to begin or revise a program — a continuum: Beginner, Intermediate, Advanced.
How It Works
Classified by training length, session frequency, intensity, and technique experience.
Beginner: <2 mo, <1–2x/wk. Intermediate: 2–6 mo, 2–3x/wk. Advanced: ≥1 yr, ≥3–4x/wk.
Training Implication
Status determines rest-day spacing, exercise complexity, and safe use of 1RM testing.
Topic 02 — Needs Analysis · 3 of 4

Physical Testing & Evaluation

What It Is
1RM testing shows max load lifted — but not rapid force-generating capacity on its own.
How It Works
Combine with isometric mid-thigh pull + slow SSC (countermovement jump) + fast SSC (rebound jump) via force plates.
Compare results to normative/league or squad data to find strengths/weaknesses.
Training Implication
e.g. good CMJ height but average reactive strength index → suboptimal force profile → emphasize maximal strength block first.
Topic 02 — Needs Analysis · 4 of 4

Primary Training Goal by Sport Season

What It Is
Testing results + the sport's movement/physiological analysis + season priorities set ONE primary goal per block.
How It Works
Off-season: hypertrophy/endurance → strength/power later. Preseason: sport-specific. In-season: maintenance. Postseason: active rest.
Emphasis/de-emphasis approach: target one quality, maintain the rest.
Training Implication
Team sports often need a multitargeted approach: train compatible qualities together (strength-power + speed), keep incompatible pairs apart (speed + cardio endurance).

Topic 03 — Exercise Selection · 1 of 4

Core vs Assistance Exercises

What It Is
Core: large muscle areas, multijoint (e.g. squat, bench press, bent-over row) — top priority.
Assistance: smaller areas, single-joint (e.g. curls, extensions) — lower priority.
How It Works
Core exercises apply directly to the sport + carry stronger dynamic correspondence (transfer to performance).
Assistance exercises are commonly used for injury prevention and rehab of a specific muscle/area.
Training Implication
Shoulder external rotators for overhand throwers, or reconditioning after a quad contusion — targeted assistance work.
Topic 03 — Exercise Selection · 2 of 4

Structural & Power Exercises

What It Is
Structural: loads the spine directly (back squat) or indirectly (power clean) — needs postural stabilization.
Power: a structural exercise done quickly and explosively.
How It Works
Traditional structural lifts (squat, bench) spend much of their ROM decelerating — use ballistic tasks (loaded jumps) for true power.
Weightlifting derivatives can emphasize a velocity- OR force-dominant stimulus.
Training Implication
In weak or low-training-age athletes, strength training alone can boost power as much as or more than ballistic training.
Topic 03 — Exercise Selection · 3 of 4

Movement Specificity & Muscle Balance

What It Is
Movement pattern, joint ROM, and muscles used should match the sport → dynamic correspondence.
Maintain strength balance between agonist and antagonist (e.g. quads vs hamstrings).
How It Works
Back squat over leg press for jump-sport athletes: erect posture, weight-bearing, full hip extension.
Isokinetic strength imbalances (e.g. weak hamstrings) → add targeted exercises to restore ratio.
Training Implication
Balance isn't always equal strength — it's the right strength/power/endurance RATIO between opposing groups.
Topic 03 — Exercise Selection · 4 of 4

Practical Constraints

What It Is
Technique experience, equipment availability, and session time all shape the final selection.
How It Works
No Olympic barbell → power clean unavailable. Limited plates → back squat replaced by front squat.
Machine leg press is faster than a free weight lunge — the time saved can buy more exercises/sets.
Training Implication
A less sport-specific but faster exercise can be worth the trade-off when time is tight.

Topic 04 — Training Frequency · 1 of 4

Training Status

What It Is
Beginner: 2–3x/week. Intermediate: 3–4x/week. Advanced: 4–7x/week.
How It Works
General guideline: 1–3 rest days between sessions that stress the same muscle groups.
Split routines (intermediate/advanced) train different muscle groups on different days → near-daily training, recovery intact.
Training Implication
No hypertrophy/strength difference between split and total-body routines at equal volume — but splits may allow more volume-load per muscle group.
Topic 04 — Training Frequency · 2 of 4

Sport Season

What It Is
Off-season: 4–6x/week. Preseason: 3–4x/week. In-season: 1–3x/week. Postseason: 0–3x/week.
How It Works
In-season, practice and competition cut into weight-room time.
Microdosing: split weekly volume across several short sessions when time is tight → less fatigue.
Training Implication
A well-trained athlete capable of 4+ sessions/week may still be capped at 1–3x/week in-season by the competition schedule.
Topic 04 — Training Frequency · 3 of 4

Training Load & Exercise Type

What It Is
Alternating heavy and light days lets athletes train more often.
How It Works
Upper body + single-joint exercises recover faster than lower body/multijoint exercises.
Heavy loads (≥80% 1RM) → greatest strength gains; light-moderate ballistic loads (<30% 1RM) → greatest vertical jump gains; 40%–70% 1RM → best for power.
Training Implication
This is why powerlifters may schedule only one very heavy squat/deadlift session per week.
Topic 04 — Training Frequency · 4 of 4

Other Concurrent Training

What It Is
Aerobic endurance, speed/agility/plyometric work, and sport-skill practice all add physical stress.
How It Works
Consider total physical stress across ALL training modes — not resistance training alone.
Training Implication
A packed conditioning + skill schedule may require cutting resistance training frequency to manage total load.

Topic 05 — Exercise Order · 1 of 4

Power → Other Core → Assistance

What It Is
Power exercises (snatch, hang power clean, jump squat) go first, then non-power core, then assistance.
How It Works
Power exercises demand high neuromuscular effort, skill, and fatigue fast — the athlete needs to be metabolically fresh.
Fatigued athletes use poor technique → higher injury risk.
Training Implication
Exercises programmed EARLIER in a session drive greater strength gains — later ones suffer from accumulated fatigue.
Example: Hang power clean → Back squat → Leg curl
Topic 05 — Exercise Order · 2 of 4

Lower/Upper Body Alternated

What It Is
Alternating lower and upper body exercises gives fuller recovery between sets.
How It Works
Useful for untrained athletes or tight sessions — cuts overall rest needed, maximizes rest per body area.
Lower body usually goes first — more muscle mass, greater load stress.
Training Implication
Minimal rest (20–30 s) between alternated exercises = circuit training — not ideal for max strength, but adds a conditioning effect.
Example: Back squat (lower) → Bench press (upper) → Romanian deadlift (lower) → Shoulder press (upper)
Topic 05 — Exercise Order · 3 of 4

Push/Pull Alternated

What It Is
Alternating pushing (bench, shoulder press) with pulling (row, curl) avoids hitting the same muscle twice in a row.
How It Works
Several pulling exercises in a row (pull-up, row, curl) fatigue the biceps → fewer reps in later sets.
Same effect if pushing exercises stack — all stress the triceps.
Training Implication
Ideal for novices or athletes returning to training — also used in circuit programs.
Example: Bench press (push) → Bent-over row (pull) → Shoulder press (push) → Lat pulldown (pull)
Topic 05 — Exercise Order · 4 of 4

Supersets & Compound Sets

What It Is
Superset: two exercises, OPPOSING muscles, little to no rest between (e.g. biceps curl → triceps pushdown).
Compound set: two exercises, SAME muscle group, back-to-back (e.g. barbell curl → hammer curl).
How It Works
Both are time-efficient but deliberately more demanding.
Training Implication
Not for unconditioned athletes — reserve for intermediate/advanced status.
Example (superset): Biceps curl → Triceps pushdown   |   Example (compound set): Barbell curl → Hammer curl

Topic 06 — Load & Repetitions · 2 of 4

RM Continuum by Training Goal

What It Is
Strength: ≤6 reps at ≥85% 1RM. Power: 1–5 reps depending on single/multi-effort event.
Hypertrophy: 8–12 reps (classic), but 30%–80% 1RM to failure gives similar results across that range.
Muscular endurance: ≥12 reps at light loads.
How It Works
Power loads are deliberately lighter than the %1RM-rep table predicts — technique breaks down before true muscular failure.
e.g. power reps are usually ≤5 but with loads only up to a 10RM (≈75% 1RM).
Training Implication
Low-load (25–35 reps, 30%–50% 1RM) and high-load (8–12 reps, 70%–80% 1RM) protocols give similar hypertrophy when taken to failure — heavier loads can be more time-efficient.
Topic 06 — Load & Repetitions · 3 of 4

1RM vs xRM Testing

What It Is
Actual 1RM: directly tested — for intermediate/advanced athletes with technique experience.
Estimated 1RM: from a 3RM/5RM using a prediction table.
xRM from goal reps: no estimation needed.
How It Works
Core exercises are preferred for 1RM testing — large muscle groups and multiple joints tolerate heavy loads better.
Multiple-RM load changes run about half the increments used in 1RM testing.
Training Implication
Common strategy: 1RM test core exercises, xRM test assistance exercises — matches test demand to exercise risk.
Topic 06 — Load & Repetitions · 4 of 4

Progression of Load

2-for-2 rule: if the athlete beats the rep goal by ≥2 reps on the last set, two workouts in a row → add load next session.

Athlete StatusBody AreaAbsolute Increase
Weaker / less trainedUpper body2.5–5 lb (1–2 kg)
Weaker / less trainedLower body5–10 lb (2–4 kg)
Stronger / more trainedUpper body5–10+ lb (2–4+ kg)
Stronger / more trainedLower body10–15+ lb (4–7+ kg)
Alternative: relative load increases of 2.5%–10% instead of absolute values. The 2-for-2 rule ignores technique quality — don't reward reps achieved with a breakdown in form.

Topic 07 — Volume · 2 of 4

Hypertrophy Volume

What It Is
8–12 reps per set (classic range), 3–6 sets per exercise.
How It Works
No meaningful hypertrophy difference between <60% and >60% 1RM loads when both are taken to failure.
Heavier loads (>80% 1RM) can still build hypertrophy — and target higher-threshold type II fibers.
Training Implication
Heavier-load hypertrophy work (e.g. 10×3) may carry more strength/power crossover than a traditional 3×10 — but takes longer and shouldn't be used on every exercise.
Topic 07 — Volume · 3 of 4

Muscular Endurance Volume

What It Is
≥12–15 reps per set, only 2–3 sets per exercise.
How It Works
Despite the high reps, overall volume-load isn't inflated — lighter loads and fewer sets offset it.
Training Implication
Endurance programming stays time-efficient because sets stay low even as reps climb.
Topic 07 — Volume · 4 of 4

Training Status & Set Progression

What It Is
Beginners: start with just 1–2 sets.
How It Works
Multiple-set training builds strength faster than single-set training, beyond the first few months.
Add sets gradually as the athlete adapts to a consistent program.
Training Implication
Fatigue cuts reps in later sets — a Reps-in-Reserve (RIR) approach manages this without training to failure.

Robinson et al.: 3 min inter-set rest in the back squat produced greater strength gains than 30 s rest. Longer rest → more work volume, heavier loads, greater max strength/power gains.
Topic 08 — Rest Periods · 2 of 3

Hypertrophy Rest

What It Is
2–3 min for multijoint exercises; 60–90 s for single-joint exercises.
Earlier guidance favored shorter rest (≤1.5 min) — start the next set before full recovery.
How It Works
Large-muscle-group exercises carry high metabolic demand — may need MORE recovery than the classic short-rest guidance suggests.
Longer rest (3 min) has produced greater hypertrophy than shorter rest (1 min) in some studies.
Training Implication
Rest for hypertrophy sits on a spectrum — depends on the load and which mechanism (tension, metabolic stress, damage) you're targeting.
Topic 08 — Rest Periods · 3 of 3

Muscular Endurance Rest

What It Is
Often under 30 seconds — the shortest rest period of any training goal.
How It Works
A deliberate restriction: minimal rest with light loads for many reps — matches the specificity principle for endurance.
Typical of circuit training, which alternates exercises with rest capped at 30 s or less.
Training Implication
A core exercise may still need 4 min rest at a 4RM load, while an assistance exercise in the SAME session needs only 1 min at a 12RM load — rest is set per exercise, not just per goal.
